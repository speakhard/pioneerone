The story

How it got made, and what happened next.

Six episodes, a decade of development, and what is left when both are over.

Television without television

In early 2010 my friend Bracey Smith and I decided to make a science-fiction drama. Neither of us worked in television. We had made a feature together before — The Lionshare — and given it away through a distributor called VODO, which put films out over BitTorrent. That had gone better than anything else we had tried, so we decided to do it again, at a scale we had no business attempting.

We raised $6,000 on Kickstarter. At the time, every request for money had to be followed by an explanation of what Kickstarter was. It paid for one pilot episode.

The premise was straightforward. Something falls out of the sky over North America and spreads radiation as it comes down. It looks like a terrorist attack. It is a Cold War relic, and the boy found inside it says he was born on Mars, to cosmonauts sent there by a country that no longer exists. We wrote it as a political and procedural drama that happens to have a Martian in it, rather than as science fiction with politics attached.

Everything past the initial premise had a root in something that actually happened.

Giving it away

The pilot went out on June 16, 2010, free, through VODO’s BitTorrent network. Within two weeks it had been downloaded more than two million times.

In those same two weeks, people we had never met sent us about $20,000 through a donation button. Over the following months that grew to roughly $100,000. That is not a figure of speech about goodwill; it is how the season was financed. There was no distributor advance and no network. The show existed because a large number of strangers each decided it should.

The money shot episodes two, three and four together over a single October. The remaining episodes were funded the same way, which is the reason for the long gaps between them — every few months the production stopped and asked again.

What it cost

Almost everyone worked for free. Professional cast and crew did professional work and were not paid for it, and the reason the season finished at six episodes rather than continuing is that we had used up every favor we had.

Along the way: four hard drives failed the night before a release. A car hit a deer in the rain driving back from a late shoot. For over a year the show was both our full-time job, and neither of us was being paid for that either.

The pilot won Best Drama Pilot at the New York Television Festival in 2010, and aired on NYC-TV in November 2011. The season was nominated for a Webby for Best Drama in 2012 and named a Webby honoree for writing. By that April the episodes had been downloaded more than four million times.

The sixth episode premiered at Anthology Film Archives in New York on December 5, 2011, and was released on December 13. Then it stopped.

What the press made of it

The angle was almost always the distribution rather than the show, which was fair enough — in 2010 “made for BitTorrent” was the surprising part.

The dream was right. It just wasn’t ours.

The pitch we made in 2010 was that you could make television without television: find an audience directly, get paid by them directly, skip the part where somebody with a development slate decides whether you exist. People thought that was either naive or faintly criminal, mostly because of the word BitTorrent.

That argument is over now, and our side won it. Making a show and putting it straight in front of an audience is simply how a great deal of work gets made. Nobody has to explain crowdfunding anymore. The strange part is how completely ordinary all of it became, and how quickly.

We were right about the model and wrong about what it would do for us.

What we had not understood is that the model does not, by itself, sustain anything. Two million people watched the pilot and around a hundred thousand dollars came in, and it still was not a living for two people, let alone a cast and crew. The thing that eventually stopped us was not indifference. It was that we had run the experiment successfully and it still did not add up to a second season.

The years afterward

What followed was about a decade of trying to make Pioneer One again, properly, inside the industry that had not bought it the first time.

This part is genuinely difficult to write without sounding either bitter or boastful, and it was neither. There were real opportunities. A producer in Los Angeles championed the show for years. An established television director came on and wanted to make it. There were successive versions — each larger, better funded on paper, and further from the last — developed with people considerably more accomplished than I was. Some of those rooms were the best education I have had. I would not give them back.

Nothing was made. That happens constantly and is not a scandal. The more interesting problem was the other one: with every pass through conventional development, the thing got bigger and looked more like everything else, and I gradually stopped recognizing what had made me want to write it. By the end I was defending a version of Pioneer One I did not particularly want to watch.

In hindsight I am glad the big one never got made.

I moved to Los Angeles and worked as an editor and a writer. Bracey produced an animated short and moved into virtual reality. Life did the ordinary thing of continuing.

Where that leaves it

What survived all of that is the original: six episodes, made for roughly the price of a used car, by people who were not being paid, and watched by millions of people who were not asked for anything. Whatever its limitations — and I can list them — it is the version that actually exists, and it is the one I still like.

I am looking at it again now, from a different position and with considerably more experience. What that turns into is described here, carefully, because nothing is settled.
